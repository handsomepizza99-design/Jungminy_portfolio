About Me

I am Jung-Min Yoon, a Ph.D. student in Biomechanics at Korea National Sport University (KNSU). I currently conduct research at the Motion Innovation Center under the supervision of Prof. Sang-Kyoon Park, focusing on the biomechanical characteristics of human movement.

My work spans sports biomechanics, gait analysis, wearable sensor technologies, and digital health, with the goal of improving athletic performance, preventing falls, and promoting health and well-being.

Research, for me, begins with a body that has felt every millisecond of motion — and continues with data that can give that feeling back to others.

08 / Projects

Research in Motion

Expand each project for the research question, method, and findings behind the headline.

Research Question

Do lower-limb joint kinetics drift across consecutive slide-board strokes in elite sprint speed skaters?

Methods

3D motion capture and inverse dynamics — first, middle, and last strokes compared across the training set, performed on an instrumented slide board mounted with a custom force plate.

Findings

Ankle plantarflexion moment and knee/hip extensor moments progressively decline from first to last strokes — quantifying within-session neuromuscular drift in elite skaters.

Impact

Informs fatigue-aware training load monitoring for slide-board conditioning programs.

Capture Setup & Joint Moment Data
Research Question

How does foot-direction angle (toe-in / neutral / toe-out) alter lower-limb joint mechanics during side-step cutting?

Methods

Foot angle was standardized with a protractor and marked with tape per condition; side-step distance was fixed at 240 cm. 3D motion capture recorded ankle, knee, and hip angle, angular velocity, moment, and power.

Findings

Toe-in cutting increased ankle dorsiflexion and knee flexion moments, while toe-out shifted load toward hip flexion/extension — identifying foot-angle as a modifiable cutting-mechanics variable.

Impact

Provides a biomechanical basis for footwork coaching cues aimed at reducing knee-joint loading during cutting maneuvers.

Protocol, Phase Breakdown & Joint Kinematics
Research Question

How do lower-extremity joint kinematics, kinetics, and inter-joint coordination differ between younger and older women during downhill walking?

Methods

Instrumented split-belt treadmill at controlled downhill grade, full-body 3D motion capture, EMG, force plates, paired younger and older female cohorts.

Expected Outcomes

Joint-level changes at ankle, knee, and hip during the downhill weight-acceptance phase; inter-joint coordination variability as a candidate fall-risk indicator.

Impact

Targets early biomechanical markers of age-related decline in downhill gait stability, ahead of clinical fall events.

Treadmill Data Collection — Control vs. Study Group
Research Question

Can wearable sensing and virtual-reality systems improve gait stability and neurophysiological function in middle-aged and older adults?

Methods

Year 1 (2025–2026): Compare gait between young and middle-aged adults across flat ground, uneven terrain, and visually distracting environments using wearable smartwatch and EEG measurements.
Year 2 (2026–2027): Introduce real-time head-mounted VR feedback to measure its effect on gait stability and neurophysiological response.

Goal

Build evidence-based strategies for fall prevention and personalized gait training before age-related decline becomes severe.

Impact

Funded by the National Research Foundation of Korea (NRF) — Doctoral Research Grant in Humanities and Social Sciences, two-year program.

Anticipated Questions & Answers

A self-prepared Q&A set for the slide-board sprint speed skating study — organized by theme, from study rationale to limitations and future directions.

Q.What is the main topic of this study?
This study analyzed how posture and lower-limb biomechanical characteristics change over time during slide-board movements in sprint speed skaters.
Q.Why did this study focus on 500 m speed skaters?
The 500 m event requires explosive propulsion and the ability to maintain speed in the later phase of the race, making it necessary to examine how low skating posture and push-off strategy change over time.
Q.Why was a slide board used instead of on-ice skating?
On-ice experiments have practical limitations for motion capture, ground reaction force, and EMG measurement. A slide board allows repeated skating-like push-off movements to be analyzed in a controlled laboratory environment.
Q.Can the slide board fully replace actual on-ice skating?
No. Actual skating involves blade-ice interaction, ice friction, forward gliding velocity, and aerodynamic drag. The slide board was used as a laboratory-based model to reproduce part of the skating movement.
Q.What is the main difference between this study and previous studies?
Many previous studies focused on starts, curves, or on-ice technique. This study instead quantitatively analyzed temporal changes in early, middle, and late strokes during slide-board performance.
Q.What is the purpose of this study in one sentence?
To analyze temporal changes in kinematic and kinetic variables during slide-board performance and provide useful information for improving technique in sprint speed skaters.
Q.Who were the participants in this study?
Ten male sprint speed skaters in their 20s and 30s, registered as competitive athletes with the Korean Sport & Olympic Committee.
Q.Is a sample size of 10 sufficient?
Generalization is limited, but recruiting elite sprint speed skaters is difficult, and the repeated-measures design allowed within-subject temporal changes to be analyzed meaningfully.
Q.Why were only male athletes included?
Sex differences may affect strength, posture, and stroke strategy, so only male sprint speed skaters were included to improve participant homogeneity.
Q.How long did the athletes perform the slide-board task?
For 1 minute 20 seconds. The first and last 10 seconds were excluded, and the middle 1 minute was used for analysis.
Q.Why were the first and last 10 seconds excluded?
The first 10 seconds may include movement stabilization, and the last 10 seconds may be affected by awareness of the task ending. The middle 1 minute was selected as a cleaner analysis window.
Q.Why were 0 s, 30 s, and 55 s selected for analysis?
These time points were chosen to represent the early, middle, and late strokes, allowing temporal changes during slide-board performance to be compared.
Q.Why were five strokes analyzed at each time point?
Five strokes were extracted to reduce random variation from any single stroke and obtain a more stable representative movement pattern at each time point.
Q.Why was the slide board custom-made and installed on the force plate?
A general training slide board is not suited to biomechanical analysis. It was custom-built and mounted on the force plate to measure lower-limb force and calculate joint moments and joint power.
Q.Why was the slide board fixed to the force plate?
If the board moved during the task, errors could occur in the ground reaction force data and joint moment calculations, so it was fixed to improve measurement stability.
Q.Why was the Z offset set to 8 mm?
The slide board's height was 8 mm. Since the athlete's foot applied force on the board surface rather than directly on the force plate, the Z offset was set to 8 mm to reflect the true contact surface height.
Q.What problem would occur without the Z offset?
The force application point would be calculated as if it occurred at the lower force-plate surface rather than the actual foot-contact surface, introducing small errors into CoP and joint moment calculations.
Q.How were the motion capture and force plate data synchronized?
An analog-digital board converted the force plate's analog signal into digital data and synchronized it with the motion capture data, since joint moments and power require joint position and ground reaction force on the same time axis.
Q.How was Event 1 (bar contact) defined?
As the moment when the distance between the lateral ankle marker of the dominant foot and the inner surface of the vertical bar was minimal — a more objective marker than visual identification.
Q.How was Event 2 (CoM x-axis max) defined, and why?
As the moment the center of mass moved farthest toward the dominant side after bar contact. Past this point the body begins pushing in the opposite direction — a functional transition from gliding to push-off.
Q.How was Event 3 (bar off) defined?
As the moment the dominant-side foot left the vertical bar.
Q.What were Phase 1 and Phase 2?
Phase 1 (Event 1→2) was defined as the gliding phase; Phase 2 (Event 2→3) was defined as the push-off phase.
Q.Are these phases identical to gliding/push-off in actual skating?
Not completely — actual skating involves blade-ice interaction. Here the phases were functionally defined within the slide-board movement itself.
Q.Why was a recovery phase not analyzed separately?
This study focused specifically on the interval from dominant-foot bar contact to bar off, which already spans the gliding and push-off phases.
Q.What is the advantage of this event-definition approach?
Events were anchored to actual movement characteristics — foot contact, maximum CoM displacement, and foot release — rather than dividing the stroke arbitrarily by time.
Q.Did the task reproduce the exact timing of a real 500 m race?
No — the goal was not to replicate race time, but to examine temporal changes across repeated skating-like movements.
Q.Why was one-way repeated measures ANOVA used?
The same athletes were measured repeatedly at three time points (0 s, 30 s, 55 s), making repeated measures ANOVA appropriate for testing temporal differences.
Q.What were the independent and dependent variables?
The independent variable was time condition (0 s / 30 s / 55 s); the dependent variables were the kinematic and kinetic outcomes compared across those time points.
Q.What significance level was used?
Statistical significance was set at α = .05.
Q.How should results be interpreted given the small sample?
Generalization is limited, but given the elite-athlete sample and repeated-measures design, the results remain meaningful for examining within-subject temporal change.
Q.What were the main findings?
As the task progressed, stroke time and vertical CoM position increased; ankle plantar-flexion increased, while knee and hip flexion angles and knee/hip extension moments decreased.
Q.What is the key message in one line?
Over time, athletes tended toward a more upright posture, showed reduced knee/hip contribution during push-off, and relied more on ankle movement.
Q.What does increasing stroke time mean?
Each stroke took longer in the later phase — a possible sign of reduced movement speed or decreased propulsive efficiency.
Q.What does the rise in vertical CoM position mean?
The body's center became higher — since a low posture is central to speed skating efficiency, this suggests a drift toward a more upright stance in the later phase.
Q.What does increased ankle plantar-flexion mean?
The foot moved further downward — on actual ice, excessive plantar-flexion may be associated with blade-tip contact or increased friction.
Q.What do the decreases in knee/hip flexion and extension moment mean?
Reduced flexion suggests more difficulty maintaining a low posture late in the task; reduced extension moment suggests a declining mechanical contribution from the larger lower-limb joints during push-off.
Q.Is the rise in ankle power a positive sign?
It may look positive in isolation, but paired with decreased knee/hip flexion and extension moment, it more likely reflects a compensatory strategy that leans more heavily on the ankle.
Q.What overall picture do the results support?
As the task progressed, athletes appeared to have more difficulty maintaining a low posture, showed reduced knee/hip contribution during push-off, and relied relatively more on ankle movement.
Q.Can the later-phase changes be directly attributed to fatigue?
Not directly — heart rate, blood lactate, and RPE were not measured, so the findings are more accurately described as temporal biomechanical changes rather than confirmed fatigue effects.
Q.Can deceleration be discussed even though speed wasn't measured directly?
Actual speed reduction wasn't measured, so it can't be concluded outright — but increased stroke time, higher CoM position, and decreased extension moments may relate to reduced propulsive efficiency.
Q.Can blade scraping be discussed when there was no blade on the slide board?
It wasn't directly measured, but increased ankle plantar-flexion may be associated with blade-tip contact or increased friction in an actual on-ice setting.
Q.Is a higher CoM position always negative?
Not always — but because a low posture is important in speed skating and a higher posture may increase aerodynamic drag, it was interpreted here as a possible decline in posture-maintenance ability.
Q.Does a lower joint moment mean lower muscle strength?
Not necessarily — joint moment depends on ground reaction force, joint position, and moment arm, so it's more appropriate to interpret it as a shift in push-off strategy rather than a direct strength decline.
Q.Did this study directly prove a drop in performance?
No — it identified biomechanical changes that may relate to reduced performance, without directly proving a performance decline.
Q.Were pushing force and stroke width directly measured?
The directly analyzed outcomes were joint angles, moments, and power. Pushing force and stroke width are interpretive possibilities drawn from these findings and prior literature, not direct measurements.
Q.What is the final, defensible one-line summary?
This study did not directly measure on-ice performance — it quantified temporal biomechanical changes during repeated slide-board strokes in sprint speed skaters.
Q.What is the main significance of this study?
It quantitatively analyzed temporal changes in posture and lower-limb push-off strategy during slide-board performance — turning a coaching observation into measurable variables.
Q.What can coaches take from this?
A way to check whether athletes show increased CoM height, reduced knee/hip flexion, or excessive ankle reliance during later strokes — concrete, observable markers of late-task technique drift.
Q.What training cues follow from this study?
"Maintain a low posture in the later phase," "don't push with the ankle alone," and "preserve sufficient knee and hip flexion before push-off."
Q.Can the slide board be used as an evaluation tool?
Yes — it can quantify changes in posture, CoM position, joint angles, moments, and power across repeated strokes, making it a candidate technique-monitoring tool.
Q.Could this connect to equipment or sensor development?
Yes — it could extend toward sensor-based feedback systems measuring CoM height, ankle plantar-flexion, knee/hip flexion, push-off force, and joint power in real time.
Q.What is the greatest limitation of this study?
The experiment was conducted in a laboratory slide-board environment rather than an actual on-ice environment, which involves blade-ice friction, gliding velocity, aerodynamic drag, cornering, and psychological factors the slide board cannot reproduce.
Q.Is the lack of physiological fatigue indicators a limitation?
Yes — measuring heart rate, blood lactate, or RPE would have allowed a clearer explanation of the relationship between temporal change and fatigue.
Q.Is the absence of on-ice data a limitation?
Yes — future studies should measure the same variables on ice and compare them against slide-board data.
Q.What should future studies improve?
On-ice measurement, relationships with actual race records, physiological fatigue indicators, and EMG analysis of additional muscles.
Q.What analysis would connect these results to real performance?
Relating the present findings to actual 500 m records, lap times, stroke frequency, stroke length, and push-off force.
Presentation note — Actual speed reduction, fatigue, and blade scraping were not directly measured in this study. These should be presented as interpretive possibilities grounded in the kinematic and kinetic findings, not as direct conclusions.
